# Property income - basis of assessment for individuals or partnerships 

## Basis of assessment (Cash Basis)

The amount assessable for a tax year is usually the net profit arising from the letting of all properties owned by the landlord in the tax year ending on **5 April**.

Property income can be assessed using the cash basis, or the accruals basis. However, the **cash basis is the default basis** for an individual with property income.

> The cash basis is the default basis for non-corporate bodies with property income, therefore in exam questions involving property income for **individuals or partnerships**, it should be assumed that the cash basis is used, unless a question specifically states otherwise.

The rental income and related expenses are assessable/deductible on a received/paid basis. Accordingly, the period to which the income/expense relates is irrelevant. 

## Allowable deductions 

The types of allowable expenses for property income are generally the same as those allowable under the normal rules for the assessment of trading income. 

The main rules are as follows: 

- The general rule is that to be allowable, the expenses must have been incurred wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the property business.   
  This covers items such as:

    - Insurance
    - Agents' fees and other management expenses 
    - Repairs
    - interest on a loan to acquire or improve a **let non-residential property**.

> there are special rules which apply to the **financing costs** of a let **residential property**.

**Non-residential property**

- For **non-residential property** (e.g., offices, shops, warehouses), interest on loans taken out to buy or improve the property is treated as a straightforward **deductible expense**.  
- This means it can be offset directly against rental income when calculating taxable property profits.

**Residential property**

- For **residential property**, the rules are different.  
- Since April 2020, landlords cannot deduct finance costs (like mortgage interest) in full from rental income.  
- Instead, they receive a **basic rate tax reduction (20%)** on those costs.  
- This change was introduced to restrict higher-rate taxpayers from gaining full relief on mortgage interest, aligning tax treatment more evenly across income levels.

> If the property is occupied for part of the year by the owner, any expenses relating to the private use are not allowable expenses. 

> There are special rules which apply to **capital expenditure** incurred by an **individual in a property business**.

> Relief is available for any expenditure incurred before letting commenced, under the normal **pre-trading expenditure rules**.

**pre-trading expenditure relief**, applies when a business incurs costs before it officially starts trading (or, in this case, before a property is first let).

- **Pre-trading expenditure**: These are expenses incurred before a business begins its taxable activity. For a property business, that means costs paid before the first tenant moves in.  
- **Relief availability**: UK tax law allows such expenses to be treated as if they were incurred on the first day of trading. This means they can be deducted against rental income once the letting begins.  
- **Examples**:  

  - Advertising for tenants before the property is let.  
  - Repairs or maintenance carried out to make the property suitable for letting.  
  - Professional fees (e.g., legal or agent costs) incurred before the first tenant signs.

**Solution**

|--------------------------------|----------|
| Rent received (Note 1)         | 5,000    |
| Allowable expenses:            |          |
| Redecoration                   | 200      |

> N1: Hembery received four rent payments in the year to 5 April 2026. The rent for **the quarter to 30 June 2026 was received in advance on 1 April 2026** and is therefore under the **cash basis** taxed in the tax year of receipt i.e. 2025/26.

> N2: The repairs expense incurred during March 2026 but paid in May 2026 will be deductible in the tax year 2026/27 when it is paid. 

## Financing costs - residential properties

Owners of residential property who live in their own property do not get tax relief for their mortgage costs.

Tax relief for residential buy-to-let finance costs is restricted to the basic rate of tax. Tax relief is given on let residential property finance costs at the basic rate (20%)

> Note that these rules do not apply to companies, and where interest relates to a non-residential property, such as a leased office or warehouse, it is still fully deductible from rental income as an expense of the letting business.

# Capital expenditure

Under the cash basis there is generally no distinction between capital and revenue expenditure in respect of plant and machinery and equipment for tax purposes.  

Expenditure on plant and machinery (except cars) used in a property business, such as tools used for maintenance of the property or office equipment used for running the business is an allowable deduction from income when paid.

However, this general rule does not apply to:

1. [Cars](#cars-capital-and-mileage-allowances)
2. Assets such as furniture, appliances, or televisions for tenants in a residential property, the cost of buying those items is not deductible as an expense against rental income, But provide a [specific relief](#replacement-domestic-items-relief) when these items are replaced .

> This is because the purchase of such items is treated as capital expenditure, not revenue expenditure.


Capital expenditure on land and buildings is not an allowable deduction. Therefore, it is important to distinguish between improvements (capital expenditure) and repairs (revenue expenditure).

- Repairs expenditure is allowable.
- Improvement expenditure is not allowable. 

> Repair means the **restoration** of an asset by **replacing subsidiary parts** of the whole asset, for example, replacing roof tiles blown off in a storm. If significant improvements are made to the asset beyond its original condition this will be regarded as capital expenditure. For example, taking off the roof and building another storey. 

## Cars [Capital and Mileage allowances]

For cars used in a business, you can either claim capital allowances and actual motoring costs, or use HMRC's mileage allowance rates as a simplified alternative.

- The taxpayers have a choice: either claim detailed allowances and expenses, or use the simplified mileage method.
- The mileage method is often easier for small businesses or landlords who don't want to track every individual motoring expense.
- However, for high-cost cars or heavy usage, capital allowances plus actual costs may give greater relief.

### Option 1: Capital allowances + actual costs

- If a car is used in the property or trading business, the **capital cost** of the car qualifies for **capital allowances** (covered in Chapter 8).  
- In addition, the **actual running costs**-such as petrol, insurance, servicing, repairs-are deductible as business expenses.  
- This method requires keeping detailed records of both the car's purchase and ongoing costs.


### Option 2: HMRC's approved mileage allowances

- Instead of claiming capital allowances and actual motoring costs, a simpler method is to use **HMRC's mileage rates**.  
- These rates are designed to cover both depreciation and running costs.  
- The approved rates are:

  - **45p per mile** for the first 10,000 business miles in a tax year.  
  - **25p per mile** for any miles above 10,000.  
- These rates are the same ones available to employees and sole traders when calculating allowable motoring expenses.


## Replacement domestic items relief 

When a landlord provides assets such as furniture, appliances, or televisions for tenants in a residential property, the cost of buying those items is not deductible as an expense against rental income.

Instead of allowing the initial purchase cost, the tax rules provide a **specific relief** when these items are replaced.

>  landlords cannot claim tax relief for the initial furnishing of a property.

Replacement domestic items relief applies to things like:

- Furniture (beds, sofas, wardrobes)  
- Appliances (fridges, cookers, washing machines)  
- Household items (TVs, carpets, curtains)  

The relief allows landlords to deduct the cost of replacing an item, **less any proceeds from selling the old item**, as long as the replacement is for the tenant's use in a residential property.  

Importantly, this relief only applies to **replacements**, not the original purchase.

The replacement cost allowed is limited to the cost of a **similar item**, excluding any improvement, but allowing for the **modern equivalent**. For example, if a washing machine is replaced with a washer-dryer, only the cost of a replacement washing machine would qualify for relief. 

Domestic items are those acquired for domestic use for example, furniture, furnishings, household appliances (including white goods), carpets, curtains and kitchenware. However, ***'fixtures'*** i.e. any plant and machinery that is fixed to a dwelling, including boilers and radiators are specifically excluded. 

This relief is **not available** on accommodation for which **rent-a-room relief** has been claimed.

- Rent-a-room relief is a special scheme for individuals who let furnished accommodation in their own home.
- If gross rental income is below a set threshold (£7,500 per year, or £3,750 if jointly let), the income can be completely exempt from tax.
- Alternatively, if income exceeds the threshold, the taxpayer can elect to deduct the allowance instead of actual expenses.

> if a landlord has already claimed **rent-a-room relief**, they cannot also claim other property-related reliefs (such as replacement domestic items relief or capital allowances) for the same accommodation. 
> This prevents "double relief" on the same income stream.

# Property income - Accruals basis

An individual or partnership may opt to use the accruals basis and it must be used if property income receipts **exceed £150,000**. However, in the examination it should only be used if the question specifies to do so.

Under the accruals basis the main difference is that rental income and related expenses are assessable/deductible on an accruals basis i.e. the rent receivable and the expenses payable in respect of the tax year.

If a tenant leaves without paying outstanding rent, under the cash basis the outstanding amount is never taxed. Under the accruals basis the amount receivable is taxed but the outstanding amount owed can be deducted as an expense. This **irrecoverable debt** is referred to as an **'impairment loss'** in the examination.

The other main difference is that expenditure on plant and machinery used in a property business, such as tools used for maintenance of the property or office equipment used for running the business is not an allowable deduction from income. However, capital allowances are available instead.

Capital expenditure on land and buildings is generally not allowable (as with the cash basis). However, under the accruals basis, in certain circumstances, **non-residential buildings** (not land) may be eligible for structures and buildings allowances

Other rules concerning allowable expenditure, including replacement domestic items relief and the restriction of finance costs for residential properties operate in the same way as for the cash basis.

## Example 1: Property income - Accruals basis

Aubrey owns a property that was let for the first time on **1 July 2025**. The rent of £5,000 p.a. is paid quarterly in advance: 

Aubrey paid allowable expenses of £200 in December 2025 (related to redecoration following a burst pipe in November 2025), and **£400 in May 2026** (related to repair work which **was completed in March 2026**).

**Solution**

**Property income - Accruals basis**

| Accruals basis                         | £        |
|----------------------------------------|----------|
| Rent received (9/12 × £5,000) (Note 1) | 3,750    |
| Less: Allowable expenses:-             |          |
| Redecoration                           | (200)    |

| **Property income**                    | **3,150**|

> N2: As the work was completed in March 2026 under the accruals basis the expenditure is deductible in the tax year 2025/26, the period in which the work was undertaken, and not when the expenditure is paid.


## Property business losses 

If rental income is less than the allowable expenditure, a loss arises. 

- If the landlord owns more than one property, the profits and losses on all the properties are **aggregated** to calculate the assessable income for the tax year. This effectively provides instant loss relief. 
- If there is an overall loss on all properties, the property income assessment for the tax year is **£Nil**. 
- Any **unrelieved loss** is **carried forward** indefinitely and is offset against the first available future property business profits. Losses can be relieved:
  
  - **Carried forward** against future profits.
  - **Set off** against total income or chargeable gains.
  - **Terminal loss relief** in the final year of trade.
  - If none of these apply, the loss is described as **"unrelieved."**
- **unrelieved loss** may occur because the taxpayer has insufficient income/gains to offset.

































































# Premiums received on the grant of a short lease 

A premium is a **lump sum payment** made by the tenant to the landlord in consideration for the granting of a lease. 

The grant of a lease is where the owner of a property gives the tenant the exclusive right to use the property for a fixed period of time. 

A **short lease** is a lease for a period of less than or equal to **50 years**.

**Income tax treatment**

**Part** of the premium received in respect of the granting of a short lease is assessed on the landlord as **property income** in the tax year the lease is granted. 

The amount assessable as property income is:

| Calculation                                                                 | Formula     |
|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------|
| **Premium**                                                                 |     X       |
| Less: Premium × 2% × (n - 1)                                                |    (X)      |
| **Property income**                                                         |  **X**      |


**Where::**  n = Length of lease = number of **complete** years (ignore part of a year). 

**Alternative calculation::** Property income = Premium × (51 - n) / 50.

> These formulae are not included in the tax rates and allowances provided to you in the TX examination.

## Example 1: Premiums received on the grant of a short lease

Wakana granted a **21-year lease** to Charles on 1 July 2025, for a premium of £10,500.

Calculate the **amount assessable** on Wakana as **property income** in the tax year 2025/26.

**Solution**

| **Property income**                    | £        |
|----------------------------------------|----------|
| Premium                                | 10,500   |
| Less: £10,500 × 2% × (21 - 1)          | (4,200)  |
| **Property income**                    | **6,300**|

**Alternative calculation:**

Property income = £10,500 × (51 - 21)/50 = £6,300

## Example 2: Premiums received on the grant of a short lease

Albert granted an **18-year lease** to Angeliki for £26,000, on 6 May 2025. 

Calculate the **amount assessable** on Albert as **property income** for the tax year 2025/26.

**Solution**

| **Property income**                    | £        |
|----------------------------------------|----------|
| Premium                                | 26,000   |
| Less: £26,000 × 2% × (18 - 1)          | (8,840)  |
| **Property income**                    |**17,160**|

**Alternative calculation:**

Property income = £26,000 × (51 - 18)/50 = £17,160

# Rent-a-room relief 

When a furnished room in an individual's main residence is let, a special exemption applies.

- If gross rental income is below a set threshold (**£7,500 per year**, or £3,750 if jointly let), the income can be **completely exempt from tax**.  
- Alternatively, if income exceeds the threshold, the taxpayer can **elect to deduct the allowance instead of actual expenses**.
- if a landlord has already claimed rent-a-room relief, they **cannot also claim other property-related reliefs** (such as replacement domestic items relief or capital allowances) for the same accommodation. This prevents "double relief" on the same income stream.  

The individual may choose between:

## Method 1 - Normal assessment

| Rental income received (or receivable) | £        |
|----------------------------------------|----------|
| Rental income                          | X        |
| Less: Expenses paid (or payable)        | (X)      |
| **Profit**                             | **X**    |

---

## Method 2 - Rent-a-room relief

| Rental income received (or receivable) | £        |
|----------------------------------------|----------|
| Rental income                          | X        |
| Less: Rent-a-room relief               | (7,500)  |
| **Profit**                             | **X**    |


- If more than one person receives rental income from letting accommodation in the same property (for example, a married couple jointly taking in lodgers), the £7,500 limit can be halved. Each person then has a limit of £3,750.
- In deciding whether to elect for rent-a-room relief, the key question will therefore be whether or not expenses exceed £7,500. 
- A rent-a-room relief **election must be made within one year of 31 January following the end of the tax year.** Therefore, for the tax year 2025/26, the election must be made by 31 January 2028 if the taxpayer wishes to:
  - deduct rent-a-room relief instead of actual expenses incurred, or  
  - ignore the exemption for the tax year, for example, if a loss is incurred.
